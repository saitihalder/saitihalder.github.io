Welcome to my corner of the internet! I'm Saiti Halder. I’m a healthcare strategy consultant and PhD-trained biomedical engineer working at the intersection of science, strategy, and enterprise transformation. I advise senior leaders across biopharma, MedTech, and health systems on enterprise and R&D strategy, operating model design, and large-scale transformations - from post-merger integration and ERP value cases to integrated business planning and commercial brand acceleration. My work spans enterprise strategy and deep R&D contexts, including R&D operating models, integration capability building, performance management, and value realization. I bring a strong quantitative and analytical toolkit, paired with the ability to translate complex scientific and operational issues into clear executive decisions. I hold a PhD in Biomedical Engineering, with research focused on translational cardiovascular disease models, and I enjoy roles that bridge scientific depth with strategic decision-making.

About Me
======

**Early Life**

I was born in Dhaka, Bangladesh in the midst of a very hot summer in June - when people crave rain and a cool breeze. My parents named me Saiti Srabonti which roughly translates to the coolness that prevails after a rain. They could not have named me better - like my name, I carry a calm and soothing energy even through all the ups and downs of life. Growing up, I felt a strong connection to science, business as well as the fine arts. I learned dance, music, demonstrated a strong scientific aptitude and business curiosity. This eclectic but conflicting interests is what shaped my journey. 

**Undergraduate Education**

 After finishing high school from Sunnydale School in Dhaka, I embarked on a life altering journey. I got the opportunity to study Biomedical Engineering at [Columbia University](https://www.columbia.edu/) on a full ride as a [C. Prescott Davis Scholar](https://www.cc-seas.columbia.edu/scholars/named). During my incredible 4 years here, I got the chance to embrace both my scientific and artistic sides and I danced my way through college (quite literally by being a part of [Columbia Taal](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/taal/)). I was fortunate to be mentored by [Prof. Clark T Hung](https://www.bme.columbia.edu/faculty-staff/directory/clark-t-hung) who introduced me to the world of tissue engineering. 

**Graduate Education**

After my journey ended at Columbia University, I wanted to continue my research trajectory in the field of tissue engineering and biomechanics. In 2019, I started my PhD at [Yale University](https://www.yale.edu/) under the mentorship of [Prof. Stuart Campbell](https://engineering.yale.edu/research-and-faculty/faculty-directory/stuart-campbell). My thesis, titled ["Identifying Mechanisms of Pathogenicity in Familial Cardiomyopathy-Linked Alpha-Tropomyosin Mutations"](https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2350&context=gsas_dissertations) focuses on studying the mechanisms of inherited mutations that can lead to familial cardiomyopathies. 

**Professional Experience**

During my graduate journey, I witnessed the struggles scientists often face in commercializing their inventions. I recognized that there exists a gap between great scientific discoveries and the business considerations that can make a commercial path feasible. Always having a penchant for solving business problems, I decided to pursue.

Low-thrust (LT) propulsion systems are becoming increasingly popular in space missions, both large strategic science missions (e.g., Hayabusa, Dawn, Hayabusa2, Bepi-Colombo, Lucy, Psyche) and small technology demonstration missions (e.g., Mars Cube One, NEA Scout, Lunar Flashlight), due to their characteristically high specific impulses which allow them to achieve a higher payload-to-propellant ratio than their impulsive counterparts. Due to their characteristically long thrust arcs, MTEs are quite common for LT trajectories. And, unless specifically accounted for during the preliminary mission design phase, MTEs can severely impact the mission performance, and in some cases may even result in complete mission failure depending on the remaining mission time, and the available onboard fuel. These effects can be especially dire in missions where success depends on certain maneuvers being performed at critical junctures along the trajectory (e.g., flybys). -->
